Global protected areas as refuges for amphibians and reptiles under climate change

Mi C.#, Ma L.#, Yang M., Li X., Meiri S., Roll U., et al. (# co-first)

Nature Communications 14, 1389 (2023) · Read on nature.com · DOI 10.1038/s41467-023-36987-y

Protected areas (PAs) are still the backbone of conservation policy. Whether they remain effective when climates shift is an empirical question — especially for amphibians and reptiles, whose thermal and hydric niches can be narrow and whose dispersal is often limited.

This Nature Communications study, co-first-authored by Chunrong Mi and Liang Ma, collates distributional information for more than 14,000 herpetofauna species (about 70% of described diversity) and evaluates PA coverage under contemporary and future climates using species distribution models.

Headline results (as published)

Over 91% of assessed species currently occur in PAs, a proportion projected to hold under climate change. Range losses inside PAs are lower than outside, so the share of species that are effectively protected can rise even as absolute habitats shrink. Yet more than 7.8% of species still fall outside PAs, with conspicuous gaps across tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests and across many non-high-income countries. The authors also flag extinction risks on the order of hundreds of amphibians and reptiles across the century under climate change.

PAs can be climate refuges — but only if the network is completed where tropical herpetofauna still live beyond the fence.

How APEC uses the paper

In group meetings the article is read as both a data feat and a planning argument: physiology and fine-scale vulnerability work (embryos, shade, water loss) must eventually speak to network design.
