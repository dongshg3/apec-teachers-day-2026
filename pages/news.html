Why embryos must enter the climate conversation

APEC Lab News Desk · based on Conservation Biology work led by Ma Liang's team

Teachers' Day special framing · 2025–2026 research cycle

“In my younger and more vulnerable years…” — the opening of The Great Gatsby helped spark an APEC question that is anything but literary ornament: if conservation maps are drawn from adult ecology alone, what happens to the younger and more vulnerable stages locked inside eggs?

For oviparous reptiles, embryos cannot shuttle between sun and shade. Nest microclimate is their entire climate. When mean temperatures rise and heat events stack, developmental opportunity can collapse even where adults still find activity windows. That asymmetry is now a signature theme of the APEC Lab.

From 48 species to a global EO map

In work synthesised for Conservation Biology, the team assembled embryonic developmental data across 48 oviparous reptiles spanning lizards, snakes, tuatara, turtles and crocodilians. Coupled microclimate and embryo-physiology models project egg development opportunity (EO) under +2 °C and +4 °C futures. Tropical embryos lose hours; higher latitudes may gain them — a latitudinal hinge familiar from adult studies, now quantified for the egg.

Squamate embryos develop fastest; turtles slowest. Cold tolerance strengthens with latitude; warm thermal safety margins shift with latitude and elevation. These patterns are not footnotes — they parameterise who fails first when nests overheat.

The <17% overlap problem

For five lizard species assessed for both adults and embryos, high-risk zones for the two stages overlap by less than 17% inside the species' ranges. Adult priority areas therefore cannot be assumed to shelter eggs. Climate-smart reserves that ignore nests risk protecting the wrong hectares.

Truly effective protection must cover every life stage — a scientific claim, and, this Teachers' Day, a metaphor for mentorship.

Links into the Nature record

The embryo–adult programme sits beside APEC's open-access Nature Communications papers on desert birds and herpetofauna protected areas, and beside co-authorship on a 2023 Nature assessment of illegal hunting in China. Together they argue that physiological detail and large-N spatial evidence are not rival aesthetics; they are sequential steps in the same pipeline students learn in the lab.
